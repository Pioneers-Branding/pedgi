Expert Evaluation for Pediatric & Adolescent GI Bleeding

Noticing signs of gastrointestinal bleeding in your child or teenager can be alarming. Prompt, expert subspecialty evaluation brings peace of mind and effective treatment.

Gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding in children and teenagers can originate from anywhere along the digestive tract, from the esophagus and stomach down to the small intestine and colon. Causes vary significantly by age, ranging from harmless food-color staining or constipation-related fissures to juvenile polyps, allergic colitis, or Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) in adolescents.

PEDGI provides dedicated care tailored specifically for young people from birth through age 21. Our board-certified pediatric gastroenterologists combine decades of expertise with advanced diagnostic technologies to identify the exact cause of bleeding and restore your child’s health.

Understanding GI Bleeding Across Age Groups

Infants & Toddlers (0–3 Years)

Common causes include milk protein allergy (allergic colitis), severe diaper dermatitis, or minor anal fissures from hard stools.

School-Aged Children (4–12 Years)

Frequently associated with benign juvenile polyps, infectious diarrhea (bacterial colitis), constipation fissures, or Meckel's diverticulum.

Teenagers & Young Adults (13–21 Years)

Common causes include Inflammatory Bowel Disease (Crohn’s Disease & Ulcerative Colitis), peptic ulcer disease, Mallory-Weiss esophageal tears, or pill-induced esophagitis.

Occult / Microscopic Bleeding

Invisible blood loss detected via routine stool screening or investigated due to unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, pale skin, or chronic fatigue in teens.

Signs & Symptoms

Types of Gastrointestinal Bleeding in Children

Gastrointestinal bleeding is generally categorized by location and appearance. Recognizing these symptoms helps our specialists pinpoint the source quickly.

Upper GI Bleeding

Bleeding originates in the esophagus, stomach, or upper small intestine (duodenum).

  • Vomiting bright red blood or dark "coffee-ground" material (hematemesis)
  • Dark, black, tarry-smelling stools (melena)
  • Upper abdominal burning, nausea, or indigestion

Lower GI Bleeding

Bleeding originates in the lower intestine, colon, rectum, or anus.

  • Bright red blood mixed in stool or visible on toilet paper (hematochezia)
  • Mucus or maroon-colored stools
  • Crampy lower belly pain or urgent diarrhea

Occult & Unexplained Bleeding

Slow, microscopic blood loss that is not visible to the naked eye.

  • Unexplained iron-deficiency anemia in blood tests
  • Pale skin, weakness, dizziness, or poor stamina in teens
  • Unexplained weight loss or slowed physical growth

When Should You Seek Immediate Medical Attention?

If your child or teenager experiences large amounts of bright red blood in vomit or stool, dark tarry stools, dizziness, weakness, severe sudden abdominal pain, or fainting, seek immediate emergency medical evaluation. For non-emergent GI bleeding concerns, prompt subspecialty care is essential.

Diagnostic Excellence

Advanced Diagnostics for Pediatric & Teen GI Bleeding

Accurate diagnosis is the key to safe, effective treatment. Our specialists utilize state-of-the-art, child-friendly diagnostic tools.

Comprehensive blood work checks for anemia, infection, and inflammatory markers (such as ESR and CRP). Stool tests evaluate for occult blood, calprotectin (gut inflammation), and bacterial pathogens.

When direct visualization is needed, our pediatric gastroenterologists perform upper endoscopy (EGD) or colonoscopy using specialized, child-sized instruments under dedicated pediatric sedation. This allows us to inspect mucosal tissue, take painless biopsies, or remove polyps in a single safe procedure.

For older children and teenagers with obscure small bowel bleeding, wireless video capsule endoscopy allows thorough visualization of the entire small intestine without invasive surgery.

Based on findings, treatment plans are tailored to your child’s diagnosis — ranging from dietary modifications and acid suppression to anti-inflammatory IBD therapies, endoscopic polypectomy, or infection treatment.

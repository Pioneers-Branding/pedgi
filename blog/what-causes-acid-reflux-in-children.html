Quick Answer: What Causes Acid Reflux in Children?

Acid reflux in children is primarily caused by an underdeveloped or transiently relaxing Lower Esophageal Sphincter (LES)—the ring of muscle that acts as a one-way valve between the esophagus and stomach. In infants, acid reflux is usually physiological due to an immature digestive tract and liquid diet. In toddlers and older children, reflux is commonly caused by dietary triggers (citrus, fatty/fried foods, sodas), overeating, cow's milk protein allergy, delayed stomach emptying, chronic constipation, or anatomical conditions like a hiatal hernia.

Understanding Acid Reflux vs. GERD in Pediatrics

It is common for parents to notice their baby spitting up after a feeding or to hear an older child complain of a burning chest sensation or sour taste after dinner. In pediatric medicine, understanding what causes acid reflux in children starts with distinguishing between benign, temporary reflux and clinical Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD).

Gastroesophageal Reflux (GER) is the passage of stomach contents back up into the esophagus, sometimes reaching the mouth. GER is extremely common, normal, and harmless in most healthy infants—often called "happy spitting."

In contrast, Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD) occurs when stomach acid and digestive enzymes frequently back up into the esophagus, causing troublesome symptoms or tissue inflammation. According to clinical consensus guidelines from the North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (NASPGHAN), GERD is diagnosed when reflux leads to severe pain, feeding refusal, poor weight gain, esophagitis, or respiratory complications.

Clinical Key Point: GER vs. GERD

Spitting up without crying or weight loss is usually uncomplicated GER. However, if spitting up is accompanied by painful screaming, back arching, food avoidance, or poor growth, your child may be suffering from GERD, requiring specialized evaluation by a pediatric gastroenterologist.

Why Infants Get Acid Reflux (Anatomical & Developmental Causes)

During the first year of life, more than 50% of infants experience some degree of acid reflux, peaking between 2 and 4 months of age. Parents frequently ask: why is reflux so prevalent in babies?

The primary anatomical and physiological factors that cause infant acid reflux include:

  • Immature Lower Esophageal Sphincter (LES): The LES is a ring of smooth muscle located at the junction of the esophagus and stomach. In newborns, this valve is still developing and relaxes spontaneously (called transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxations or TLESRs), allowing stomach milk and digestive acid to travel backward.
  • Liquid Diet Volume: Relative to their body weight, infants consume a massive volume of liquid (breast milk or infant formula) each day. If an adult drank the proportional equivalent, it would equal nearly 2 gallons of fluid per meal!
  • Recumbent Posture: Infants spend most of their hours lying flat on their backs for sleeping and playing, eliminating gravity's natural assistance in holding food down in the stomach.
  • Short Esophageal Length: A baby's esophagus measures only a few inches long, meaning gastric fluid needs to travel only a tiny distance to reach the back of the throat or mouth.

For the vast majority of babies, as their digestive tract matures, they sit upright, and solid foods are introduced, infant reflux naturally resolves on its own by 12 to 14 months of age.

What Causes Acid Reflux in Toddlers & Older Children?

While infant reflux is primarily developmental, acid reflux in toddlers, young children, and adolescents is often caused by a combination of physical factors, dietary habits, and lifestyle influences.

When evaluating what causes acid reflux in older children, pediatric specialists investigate several physiological mechanisms:

A. Transient Lower Esophageal Sphincter Relaxation (TLESR)

Even in older children with fully formed anatomy, the LES muscle can loosen or open at inappropriate times—unrelated to swallowing. Large meal sizes, stomach distension, and high-fat foods trigger neural reflexes that cause the sphincter to stay open longer than normal.

B. Increased Intra-Abdominal Pressure

Any condition that pushes upward against the stomach increases internal pressure, forcing acid up past the LES into the esophagus:

  • Chronic Constipation: Stool backup in the colon creates pressure in the abdomen, slowing stomach emptying and exacerbating acid reflux. (Learn more on our Educational Resources page).
  • Pediatric Obesity: Excess abdominal adiposity exerts constant pressure on the stomach cavity.
  • Chronic Coughing or Wheezing: Recurrent coughing fits from asthma or allergies compress the stomach.
  • Tight Waistbands: Clothing that squeezes the midsection tightly.

C. Delayed Gastric Emptying (Gastroparesis)

When a child's stomach empties food into the small intestine too slowly, food and acid sit in the stomach for prolonged periods. This increased volume stretches the stomach walls, triggering reflux events.

Key Dietary & Lifestyle Triggers

Diet plays a central role in triggering or worsening acid reflux in children. Certain foods and eating behaviors either relax the LES valve directly or increase stomach acidity.

Common Trigger Foods in Children:

  • Citrus Fruits & Juices: Oranges, grapefruits, lemons, and tomatoes contain high natural acid content that irritates the esophageal lining.
  • High-Fat and Fried Foods: French fries, fried chicken, cheese, and heavy gravies delay stomach emptying, keeping acid in the stomach longer.
  • Chocolate & Peppermint: Contain natural chemical compounds (methylxanthines) that relax the muscle tone of the LES valve.
  • Carbonated Beverages & Sodas: Introduce gas bubbles into the stomach, causing bloating, belching, and acid regurgitation.
  • Caffeinated Foods & Drinks: Energy drinks, tea, and caffeinated sodas stimulate excess gastric acid secretion.
  • Spicy Foods: Chili, hot peppers, and heavy spices irritate sensitive esophageal tissue.

Eating Behaviors That Cause Reflux:

  • Overeating or Eating Too Fast: Stretches the stomach capacity beyond comfortable limits.
  • Eating Late at Night: Going to sleep with a full stomach within 2 to 3 hours of eating removes gravity's help, allowing acid to flow into the throat while sleeping.
  • Vigorous Physical Play After Meals: Jumping, running, or tumbling immediately after eating sloshes stomach contents upward.

Underlying Medical Conditions Linked to GERD

When acid reflux is persistent, severe, or resistant to standard dietary modifications, an underlying medical condition may be the primary root cause.

1. Cow's Milk Protein Allergy (CMPA) & Food Intolerances

According to guidelines published by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), up to 30% of infants evaluated for severe reflux actually have non-IgE mediated cow's milk protein allergy. Immune inflammation in the gut causes dysmotility, vomiting, and reflux symptoms that mimic GERD.

2. Eosinophilic Esophagitis (EoE)

EoE (NIDDK Overview) is an allergic condition where white blood cells called eosinophils build up in the esophagus, typically triggered by food allergies (such as milk, wheat, soy, or eggs). EoE causes swallowing difficulty, food impaction, chest pain, and refractory acid reflux symptoms in children.

3. Hiatal Hernia

A hiatal hernia occurs when the upper part of the stomach pushes upward through the diaphragm muscle into the chest cavity. This anatomical abnormality disrupts the normal pressure barrier of the lower esophageal sphincter, making acid reflux almost inevitable.

4. Neurological Impairments & Developmental Delays

Children with neurological conditions (such as cerebral palsy or developmental delay) often experience impaired digestive motility, abnormal muscle tone in the esophagus, and prolonged recumbency, placing them at much higher risk for chronic severe GERD.

Recognizing Symptoms: How Reflux Presents Across Age Groups

Because infants and young toddlers cannot express "heartburn" in words, acid reflux symptoms vary significantly depending on your child's age:

Age Group Common Acid Reflux Symptoms Behavioral Clues to Watch For
Infants (0–12 Months) Frequent wet burps, spitting up, arching of back during feedings (Sandifer syndrome), wet coughing. Irritability during or right after feeding, pulling away from bottle/breast, refusal to lie flat on back.
Toddlers (1–3 Years) Regurgitation, bad breath (halitosis), night waking, poor weight gain, chronic throat clearing. Holding neck/chest, refusal of solid foods, demands for liquids to soothe throat pain.
Older Children & Teens Heartburn, chest burning, sour taste in mouth, regurgitation, difficulty swallowing (dysphagia). Complaints of "food stuck in throat", morning nausea, vocal hoarseness, chronic cough or asthma flare-ups.

How Pediatric Gastroenterologists Diagnose Acid Reflux

At PEDGI Tampa Bay, our pediatric gastroenterologists combine decades of specialized clinical experience with compassionate diagnostic tools to pinpoint the exact cause of your child's reflux.

Diagnostic approaches may include:

  • Detailed Clinical History & Dietary Diary: Evaluating feeding schedules, growth charts, symptom timing, and elimination trials.
  • Targeted Elimination Trials: Testing a 2 to 4 week trial of hypoallergenic formula (amino-acid based) or milk elimination under medical supervision.
  • Upper GI Endoscopy (EGD) with Biopsies: Performed under pediatric sedation, a thin flexible camera examines the lining of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum to test for esophagitis, EoE, infection, or hiatal hernia.
  • 24-Hour pH / Impedance Monitoring: Measures both acidic and non-acidic reflux episodes over 24 hours to quantify reflux severity and correlate symptoms with acid events.
  • Upper GI Barium Series: Fluoroscopic X-ray imaging to check for anatomical blockages, malrotation, or strictures.

Evidence-Based Treatments & Home Remedies

Management of pediatric acid reflux is always tailored to your child's specific age, symptom severity, and underlying diagnosis.

A. Home Remedies & Lifestyle Modifications

  • Post-Feeding Positioning: Keep infants upright (held vertically) for 20 to 30 minutes after every feeding. Avoid placing infants in car seats or infant swings immediately after eating, as bent posture increases abdominal pressure.
  • Formula & Feeding Adjustments: Offer smaller, more frequent meals to prevent stomach overdistension. Under a pediatrician's guidance, formula may be thickened with rice cereal or commercial thickeners.
  • Dietary Elimination for Older Kids: Remove citrus, soda, fried foods, chocolate, and caffeine. Ensure the last meal or snack is completed 3 hours before bedtime.
  • Address Constipation Promptly: Promoting regular bowel movements reduces upward abdominal pressure on the stomach.

B. Pediatric Medical Therapies

When conservative measures are insufficient and GERD is causing painful esophagitis, tissue damage, or sleep disruption, your pediatric gastroenterologist may prescribe safe, weight-adjusted medications:

  • Histamine-2 Receptor Antagonists (H2 Blockers): Such as famotidine, which reduce stomach acid production.
  • Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Such as omeprazole, lansoprazole, or esomeprazole, which provide stronger and longer-lasting acid suppression to allow inflamed esophageal tissue to heal.
  • Prokinetic Agents: In select cases of delayed stomach emptying, medications to accelerate gastric transit may be evaluated.

Red Flag Warning: When to See a Specialist

While most acid reflux in children is manageable, certain warning signs indicate a more serious condition requiring prompt medical attention from a specialist.

Red Flag Warning Signs

Contact a pediatric gastroenterologist immediately if your infant or child experiences:

  • Green or yellow (bile-stained) vomit or persistent forceful projectile vomiting
  • Blood in vomit (looks like bright red blood or dark coffee grounds)
  • Blood in stool or dark tarry black stools
  • Failure to thrive, weight loss, or dropping on growth charts
  • Severe pain or difficulty swallowing (feeling like food is stuck)
  • Choking, gagging, wheezing, or recurrent pneumonia
  • Extreme, inconsolable crying during or after every meal
